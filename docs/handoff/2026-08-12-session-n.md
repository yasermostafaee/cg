## The defect, and what caused it

You saw it on the real canvas: with the playhead at the in-point a Lottie showed its poster frame —
a mid-clip picture — while **every other frame was correct**, and scrubbing away and back never
healed it.

**The cause was not a race, not a dropped paint, and not a falsy-zero guard.** It was an explicit
branch in `LottieDriver.positionAt`, which session M added:

```ts
if (outroElapsedMs === null && introElapsedMs <= 0) {
  this.paint(this.o.posterFrame ?? this.o.introEnd);
  return;
}
```

So the brief's step-1 experiment (instrument `goToFrame`, split "the call never happens" from
"lottie-web ignores it") was not needed, and one of its "already verified, do not re-derive" bullets
was false in the current tree — `positionAt` did not reach the mapping at zero elapsed at all. I am
flagging that rather than quietly working around it, because the bullet was written against
`e44afbf0` and the tree is four commits past it.

🔴 **Your own observation is what rules out the other two shapes, and it is worth keeping as a
habit: the fault was not history-dependent.** A race loses to whichever paint lands last and heals
on the next scrub; a swallowed zero leaves the previous frame, not one specific unrelated frame.
Only a deterministic function of the frame reproduces on every visit — which points at a branch, not
at timing. That inference cost nothing and was decisive.

## Why the branch was there, and why it is gone

It was not carelessness, which is why it is worth one paragraph. Session M's first implementation
mapped every frame faithfully; the Linux `e2e` job failed it on two D-125 tests asserting a placed
Lottie renders a VISIBLE frame on the canvas, because at the in-point the honest frame is `ip` —
where a build-on clip has scaled the graphic to nothing. Reading that as "a living requirement
outranks an implementation's convenience", session M made the in-point rest on the poster. It went
green, and it put the defect you found on the canvas.

Your decision settles it: **the mapping wins at every frame.** D-125's poster had two stated
reasons; they did not age the same way, and both are now annotated at `poster()` in place:

- _"a design surface that never plays"_ — **dead.** The playhead drives the canvas.
- _"both ends of a furniture clip are commonly blank"_ — **still true, and unactionable.** Acting on
  it needs an "at rest" exception, and the canvas cannot know parked-from-playing: it receives one
  `scrub` stream and nothing else. The only available approximation is frame-based, which is exactly
  the branch that broke — and which would also flash the poster for one frame on every PLAY from the
  top.

**`driver.poster()` is KEPT** (you asked). It is now a PRE-TICK transient: the canvas's first tick
overwrites it, and the broadcast surfaces are blank (`cg-pending`) until `play()`, so it is never
seen there. It stays because a host that builds a runtime and never ticks still needs a defined
first frame — not because the canvas needs a poster. Had the cause been an ordering race, deleting
it would have been the right fix; it was not a race, so that reasoning does not fire.

## 🔴 Why 790 green tests did not catch it — the finding that outlives the bug

The tests were not missing. **One of them asserted the wrong rule, deliberately.** Session M wrote
`D-125 SURVIVES: at or before the in-point the canvas rests on the POSTER` and it passed, faithfully,
for the whole time the canvas was wrong. That is the failure mode to watch for: not an absent test
but a test that ENCODES a decision, which then makes the decision un-observable when it turns out to
be wrong. A suite cannot falsify a rule it was written to protect.

Two things now make the boundary observable, and they are different in kind:

1. **Unit tests use a MARKER-LESS fixture**, so `posterFrame` is the clip MIDPOINT and can never
   coincide with `ip`. With `phases` present the two collide and the assertion passes vacuously —
   which is precisely how the boundary went unnoticed. Every boundary assertion is paired with an
   `in + 1` control that proves it discriminates.
2. **The canvas E2E reads the RENDERED bbox in a real browser**, not what the driver was asked to
   paint. A spy on `goToFrame` passes whether or not lottie-web honoured the call; only the render
   can tell "asked for frame 0" apart from "shows frame 0". The two D-125 E2E tests were
   **retargeted, not deleted** — what they were really protecting (a real player renders real
   content the operator can see) is preserved, now one scrub away.
